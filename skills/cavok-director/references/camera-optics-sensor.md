# Camera Optics & Sensor Behavior

## Principle

Camera language is not only focal length. Define the perceptual and physical recording behavior that explains depth, motion, focus, highlights and imperfections.

## Decision chain

Story need → camera distance/position → field of view → sensor/format behavior → lens family → aperture / depth → focus strategy → shutter / motion → filtration / flare → exposure and highlight response.

## Sensor / format

Sensor size changes field of view at a given focal length and influences practical depth-of-field choices. Do not use format as prestige shorthand. Choose it for desired field of view, camera distance, depth, low-light behavior and production constraints.

## Focal length and distance

Perspective is primarily set by camera position. A wide lens used close exaggerates near/far relationships; a long lens usually requires greater distance and compresses visible depth. State the perceptual goal before millimeters.

## Aperture / T-stop

Choose depth of field from information needs. Deep focus supports ensemble geography and suspense inside the frame; shallow focus restricts knowledge and isolates detail. Do not default every cinematic shot to extremely shallow focus.

## Focus behavior

Define subject plane, pull target, speed, delay and landing. Human-operated focus may lag sudden movement slightly before correcting. Rack focus should transfer information or power, not decorate the shot. Avoid focus pumping and impossible instantaneous shifts.

## Focus breathing

Some lenses change framing during focus pulls. Use subtle breathing only when consistent with the lens character; do not invent large zoom-like breathing unless intended.

## Spherical vs anamorphic

Use spherical for flexible, relatively neutral geometry. Use anamorphic characteristics only when the project calls for wider horizontal rendering, oval/characterful bokeh, specific flare and edge behavior. Do not add blue horizontal flares merely because a shot is 'cinematic'.

## Distortion

Wide lenses may show barrel/edge stretching; long lenses typically reduce perspective exaggeration. Keep face distortion consistent with camera distance and framing. Avoid warping faces at the edge unless intentionally embodied.

## Bokeh

Bokeh shape and texture depend on aperture, lens design and highlight geometry. Match foreground/background defocus to depth; do not overlay identical circular bokeh dots across the image.

## Shutter and motion blur

Normal live-action motion should retain believable shutter blur. Shorter shutter creates harder, staccato motion; longer shutter increases smear and subjective blur. Use shutter character intentionally for action, memory, panic or stylization. VFX must match it.

## Rolling shutter / sensor stress

Fast pans, vibration or impacts may create subtle skew/wobble on rolling-shutter systems, but it is not a default realism filter. Use only when the camera/sensor context supports it.

## Highlight response

Define clipping/roll-off behavior for sun, fire, reflections and emissive VFX. Small highlights may clip softly while skin and important material retain detail. Avoid digital hard-white patches unless the exposure intentionally overloads.

## Filtration and halation

Diffusion may soften high-frequency detail, bloom highlights and reduce digital harshness. Keep strength consistent across shots. Halation should follow high luminance, not trace every edge.

## Lens flare / ghosts

Flare requires a bright source at an optical angle that can enter the lens. It moves with source/camera geometry. Do not add random flare when no source is present.

## Camera support interaction

Optics and support interact: handheld changes focus stability and parallax; dolly preserves smoother geometric travel; long lenses amplify small operating errors. Design focus and stabilization accordingly.

## Optical continuity checklist

- camera distance and perspective consistent across intended matches;
- depth of field preserves required information;
- focus pulls have motivated targets and realistic timing;
- shutter/motion blur matches action and VFX;
- distortion, flare, grain and halation remain coherent;
- highlights and skin respond consistently across cuts;
- no lens artifact exists without optical cause.
