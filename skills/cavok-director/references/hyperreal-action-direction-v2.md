# CAVOK Hyperreal Action Direction v2

## Purpose

This module governs live-action-feeling and hyperreal CG combat when the goal is cinematic action rather than flat coverage. It exists to prevent a recurring failure mode: technically readable fights that feel like side-scrolling gameplay because performers stay on one lateral axis, cameras remain safely outside the action, framing is dominated by medium-wides, and camera moves are added as decoration instead of being caused by performance and force.

The system unifies **character performance, action choreography, three-dimensional staging, camera choreography, optics, environment interaction, contact, and consequence**.

Core law:

> Camera does not decorate action. Camera inherits tension from performance, is triggered by action, and resolves with the action's consequence.

Chinese working law:

> **人物起势，镜头才起势；人物改变空间，镜头才改变空间。**

## Activation

Load this module for:

- hyperreal CG fights;
- live-action-feeling virtual production;
- grounded JRPG combat rendered with photographed-world logic;
- sword fights, melee, martial arts, superpower action, creature fights, pursuit-combat hybrids;
- any action sequence that must feel cinematic, dimensional, performative, and deliberately photographed.

Do not use it as the primary grammar for pure 2D anime combat. Use `2d-anime-combat-grammar.md` for that medium.

## Unified decision chain

Design action and camera as one coupled event:

```text
Character Intent
→ Performance Tell
→ Tactical Decision
→ Action Preparation
→ Camera Trigger
→ 3D Spatial Choreography
→ Action Burst
→ Camera Choreography
→ Contact / Near Miss
→ Force Transfer
→ Consequence
→ Camera Settle / Reframe
→ New Tactical State
```

Never design a camera move merely because the scene is tense, fast, or stylish.

## 1. Performance generates camera tension

Before movement, find the smallest visible signal that the character has decided to act:

- eye-line shift;
- breath change;
- jaw setting;
- support foot loading;
- shoulder compression;
- hand touching a weapon;
- pelvis changing angle;
- stance narrowing or widening;
- attention moving from face to weapon hand or escape route.

The camera should normally remain still or restrained until one of these signals creates a reason to move.

### Generic push-in prohibition

Do not open combat with a sudden close push-in merely to announce tension.

A push-in requires a trigger such as:

- recognition;
- commitment to attack;
- discovery of a threat;
- realization of a weakness;
- relationship shift;
- a decision becoming irreversible.

If no such change occurs, let performance carry the tension.

## 2. Action–Camera Coupling

Every major beat must define both the performer chain and the camera chain.

### Performer chain

```text
Intent → Tell → Load → Launch → Travel → Contact / Miss → Recovery → New State
```

### Camera chain

```text
Observe → Trigger → Launch → Follow / Yield / Cross / Hold → Contact Behavior → Consequence Behavior → Settle
```

The two chains should interlock, not mirror each other mechanically.

### Relative timing relationships

Use deliberately:

- **Character leads, camera follows:** best for surprise, realism, predatory speed.
- **Character and camera launch together:** best for committed pursuit or already-established high-speed movement.
- **Camera yields before the body arrives:** only when the audience already understands the incoming vector and the move creates space for impact.
- **Character moves, camera stays still:** best for extreme speed, sudden exits, ruthless efficiency, or readable contact.
- **Character and camera move in opposite directions:** strengthens relative velocity, pressure, or distance expansion.

Avoid automatic lockstep following.

## 3. Three-dimensional action staging

A cinematic fight must use more than left-right movement.

Design four simultaneous vectors:

1. **X axis — lateral:** left / right.
2. **Y axis — depth:** near / far, toward / away from camera.
3. **Z axis — vertical:** high / low, stairs, ramps, jumps, falls, elevation changes.
4. **Camera vector:** the camera's relation to the attack line.

### Anti-side-scroller law

Unless intentionally stylized, do not stage two consecutive major attack beats as pure lateral side views.

Across a sequence, vary:

- lateral attack;
- oblique attack into depth;
- near-to-far travel;
- far-to-near approach;
- foreground pass;
- camera-side near miss;
- vertical displacement;
- diagonal cross-frame attack;
- attack emerging from behind foreground cover.

Characters must travel through depth; the camera cannot manufacture dimensionality alone.

## 4. Shot function before shot size

Every action shot should have one dominant function:

- **Geography Shot:** establishes distance, elevation, obstacles, exits, attack axis.
- **Performance Shot:** reads intention, fear, confidence, strategy, decision.
- **Threat Shot:** makes danger approach or dominate the frame.
- **Mechanics Shot:** clearly shows a critical body or weapon mechanism.
- **Immersion Shot:** puts the camera physically near the action path.
- **Impact Shot:** prioritizes contact and force transfer.
- **Consequence Shot:** shows what the hit changed.
- **Reorientation Shot:** rebuilds geography after speed, occlusion, or axis change.

Do not make every shot a Mechanics Shot. That creates instructional-video or fighting-game flatness.

## 5. Framing rhythm and spatial breathing

Use scale change to make the fight breathe:

```text
Wide / far
→ close performance
→ deep action space
→ impact
→ consequence wide
```

Do not keep the same spatial compression for an entire exchange.

A fight should periodically expand and contract the viewer's perceived distance.

### Useful lens families

- **18–24mm:** aggressive depth, foreground passes, near-lens attacks, high spatial energy.
- **28–35mm:** primary dynamic action space.
- **40–65mm:** relationship compression, resistance, bind, decision, short tactical pauses.
- **85mm+:** remote threat, psychological isolation, surveillance, selective danger.

A shot may evolve internally if the move is motivated; one shot does not have to preserve one static composition throughout.

## 6. Camera participation levels

Choose a participation level per beat.

### Level 0 — Observer

Camera watches. Use for geography, setup, clean mechanics, brutal objective consequences.

### Level 1 — Reactive

Character moves first; camera responds. Use for realism, unpredictability, sudden attacks.

### Level 2 — Embedded

Camera lives inside the action corridor: over-shoulder pursuit, weapon pass, near-body crossing, depth travel, foreground obstruction.

### Level 3 — Subjective / Kinetic

Camera briefly shares bodily pressure or imbalance: retreat under attack, side-step with dodge, loss of equilibrium, body-rig-like surge. Use sparingly.

High participation is not automatically better.

## 7. Camera flair levels

Separate readability from showmanship.

### Flair 0 — Functional

Purely clarifies story, space, or mechanics.

### Flair 1 — Designed

Foreground reveal, subtle reframe, motivated low angle, reaction pan, controlled push.

### Flair 2 — Action choreography

Weapon pass, body pass, axial rush, ground skim, motivated axis crossing, foreground wipe, perspective handoff.

### Flair 3 — Hero Camera Move

A memorable camera event built specifically around one signature action. It may combine several techniques, but must have one clear causal spine.

Do not assign Flair levels by duration or a fixed count. Build hierarchy from dramatic load, action complexity, geography, medium, and the sequence's contrast needs. A passage may contain no Hero Camera Move, one carrying event, or—when the form genuinely supports it—more than one separated hero event. Do not make every shot a stunt, but do not remove a justified expressive candidate merely to satisfy a restraint quota.

## 8. Camera stunt grammar

### Weapon Pass Transition

A weapon passes very near lens and creates a motivated occlusion. Use the occlusion to change position, axis, focal relationship, or shot size. The weapon must physically travel through that region of space.

### Body Pass Reframe

A performer crosses near lens; the camera pans or rotates after the body passes, revealing the next attack relationship. Useful for speed and dimensionality.

### Ground-Skim Launch

Camera begins near the ground. A footstep, landing, or launch passes close and triggers camera acceleration upward or forward. The camera is started by the body's force, not by editorial anticipation.

### Axial Subject Rush / Yielding Retreat

Attack travels toward camera. Camera retreats more slowly than the performer, causing rapid scale increase. The final strike may pass lens or force a lateral escape. This is not a camera crash-in: a true crash-in is a rapid physical camera advance toward the subject.

### Vertical Rise / Dive

Camera changes height with a real vertical action beat: jump, drop, fall, descending strike, elevated enemy, collapsing level. Avoid decorative crane movement unrelated to body height.

### Reverse Pull

At or immediately after impact, camera pulls against the victim's travel, rapidly expanding visible distance and emphasizing launch force.

### Foreground Occlusion / Hidden Cut

Use pillars, vehicles, cloth, bodies, smoke, debris, weapon sweeps, walls, or flashes as motivated cover to reset camera position, focal length, axis, or generation segment. The new position must preserve causal motion.

### Motivated Axis Crossing

Cross the axis only when the audience witnesses how it happens: a performer carries the camera across, the camera arcs around a clearly anchored subject, a foreground transition reveals the new side, or the attack itself rotates the relationship. Re-establish direction after crossing.

### Vector Match Cut

Preserve force direction across a cut. A sword exits frame on a diagonal; the next shot continues the same vector through body displacement, debris, or defensive motion.

### Shot-inside-Shot

Allow one shot to evolve through several compositional states when the action motivates it: close performance → widening chase → near-lens pass → new two-shot → contact settle. Do not assume one shot equals one static framing.

## 9. Camera timing as choreography

Camera has its own action rhythm:

```text
Hold
→ Character Tell
→ Camera Trigger
→ Camera Launch
→ Chase / Yield / Cross
→ Decelerate
→ Contact Behavior
→ Settle / Release
```

The camera should often decelerate before the most important contact so the audience can read force transfer.

### Contact stability law

Fast action does not require a fast camera at the exact impact frame.

Often:

> the performer gets faster while the camera becomes clearer.

Use stable or nearly stable contact when it improves force, geometry, or emotional brutality.

## 10. Camera response to impact

Do not default to shake.

Possible impact responses:

- no movement at all;
- short delayed bump when force reaches camera support;
- tiny translation rather than random shake;
- focus or foreground disturbance;
- recoil movement tied to subjective POV;
- hard settle after preceding motion;
- reverse pull to reveal displacement.

Camera response must occur because force reaches the camera's physical or subjective position.

## 11. Environment as camera partner

Environment must participate in staging, not remain decorative background.

Use:

- columns and doorways for reveals;
- vehicles or creatures as foreground wipes;
- stairs, ramps, ledges, rails, debris, furniture, terrain and walls to create Z-axis and depth changes;
- smoke, steam, dust, rain, cloth, sparks and debris for layered depth and motivated occlusion;
- reflective or translucent surfaces only when they add information or tension.

A strong action environment changes how performers and camera move.

## 12. Perspective choreography

The body and camera should sometimes cooperate to create controlled perspective exaggeration.

Examples:

- fist, foot, or blade approaches near lens and rapidly changes scale;
- defender's shoulder anchors foreground while attacker expands from deep space;
- weapon thrust defines a strong near-far line;
- character crosses from oversized foreground to small deep-background silhouette.

Perspective exaggeration must remain tied to actual camera position and performer trajectory in hyperreal mode.

## 13. Character-specific camera combat signature

Different fighters should not receive the same camera language.

Define for each major combatant:

```text
Performance baseline:
Preferred tactical range:
Movement character:
Camera lead/lag tendency:
Preferred participation level:
Preferred lens/depth behavior:
Allowed camera stunts:
Impact behavior:
Settle behavior:
```

Examples:

- calm expert: long holds, performer leads, camera reacts late, impact becomes very stable;
- berserker: camera yields, foreground is disrupted, axial pressure and heavy consequence framing;
- speed fighter: body passes, occlusion transitions, whip reacquisition, deep-space crossings;
- elegant fighter: smooth arcs, precise reframes, clean perspective handoffs, minimal shake.

Camera language should reveal how the character fights.

## 14. Action flair and camera flair are separate

Score independently:

- **Action Flair:** complexity and distinctiveness of choreography.
- **Camera Flair:** boldness and memorability of camera design.
- **Spatial Flair:** dimensional use of X/Y/Z axes and environment.

Do not max all three continuously.

A complex move may need a simple camera. A simple move may justify a heroic camera move.

## 15. Opening combat rule

Do not assume action should begin with camera movement.

A high-tension opening may be:

```text
Still camera
→ environmental motion only
→ performance tell
→ character launch
→ camera reacts
```

Stillness preserves headroom for later acceleration.

## 16. Recovery and consequence

The camera must show what the action changed.

After contact, protect at least one of:

- displacement;
- stance failure;
- weapon position change;
- injury;
- environmental damage;
- advantage reversal;
- separation or compression of distance;
- new elevation;
- emotional reassessment.

Do not reset both fighters to neutral immediately after a hit.

## 17. AI-video execution contract

When translating to an AI video model, write performer motion and camera motion separately but causally linked.

Bad:

```text
Dynamic cinematic camera follows the fighter as she attacks.
```

Better:

```text
Camera begins locked. The fighter loads her rear foot and starts drawing the blade; camera remains still through the preparation. Only after her first explosive step does the camera launch into a short rightward track, initially lagging half a beat. As she enters final striking distance, camera decelerates and settles into a 3/4 medium-wide so the blade contact, support feet, and force transfer are readable.
```

Specify:

- starting support / camera position;
- performer trigger;
- camera delay or lead;
- movement vector;
- acceleration curve;
- framing evolution;
- contact behavior;
- end settle;
- continuity handoff.

If the model cannot execute a complex Hero Camera Move reliably, split at a motivated occlusion or stable state rather than flattening the directing idea into generic side coverage.

## 18. Anti-flatness QC

Before approving a hyperreal action sequence, ask:

1. Are two consecutive major attacks pure lateral side views?
2. Does at least one major beat use strong depth travel?
3. Do foreground, midground, and background relationships change?
4. Does elevation change meaningfully somewhere when appropriate?
5. Does shot scale change the felt space rather than merely vary coverage?
6. Does a performer travel near-to-far or far-to-near?
7. Is there at least one shot whose main purpose is not mechanics coverage?
8. Is at least one camera move visibly triggered by a performer or event?
9. Does the environment affect either performer or camera path?
10. Does a major impact change the composition or geography afterward?
11. Is there at least one memorable Camera Flair 2–3 move in a hero action passage?
12. Does the camera ever stay still while the performer moves fast?
13. Does camera motion decelerate or clarify around a critical contact?
14. Can every conspicuous push, orbit, whip, crane, or axis crossing explain its trigger?
15. Does each major fighter have a distinct camera combat signature?

If most answers are no, the sequence is likely flat even if individual actions are technically correct.

## 19. Failure patterns

### Side-scroller syndrome

Symptoms: repeated left-right attacks, constant side-on medium-wide, minimal depth change.

Fix: redesign performer trajectories in Y/Z before adding camera movement.

### Coverage syndrome

Symptoms: every shot safely shows both full bodies and every weapon mechanic.

Fix: assign dominant shot functions; allow selective occlusion, near passes, performance shots, and consequence shots.

### Decorative camera syndrome

Symptoms: push-ins, orbits, whips, or shakes occur without a performer or story trigger.

Fix: remove the move or bind it to a visible performance/action event.

### Lockstep camera syndrome

Symptoms: camera begins, accelerates, turns, and stops exactly with the performer every time.

Fix: design lead/lag/hold/opposition relationships.

### Over-showmanship

Symptoms: every beat uses a stunt camera, geography collapses, action feels like a demo reel.

Fix: restore Flair 0–1 breathing space and reserve Flair 3 for signature beats.

### Impact blur syndrome

Symptoms: camera, motion blur, shake, VFX, and actor motion all peak at contact, hiding the hit.

Fix: decelerate or stabilize camera at contact; preserve one clean force-transfer read.

## Final principle

Hyperreal action should feel as if the characters, environment, and camera are participating in the same physical and dramatic event.

The camera may be bold, elegant, aggressive, or spectacular, but its movement must be earned by character tension, action vector, spatial transformation, or consequence.

> **The performer creates the force. The camera interprets that force. The edit preserves its direction. The consequence proves it happened.**
