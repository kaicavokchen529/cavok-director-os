## 1. Physical event model

Treat VFX as an anomalous event photographed in a real location:

**source → precursor → formation → propagation → contact → physical feedback → aftermath → dissipation**

Never substitute brightness, colored particles, or a generic explosion for physical causality.

## 2. Six design layers

### Source

Identify the exact origin: hand, mouth, weapon, surface, atmospheric volume, electrical path, or spatial boundary. Keep it stable across shots.

### Formation

Show precursors before manifestation: temperature change, pressure shift, refraction, condensation, ionization, vibration, displaced dust, altered foliage, breath, hair, or fabric.

Show matter or energy organizing over time. Avoid instant finished objects unless instantaneous formation is itself a defined rule.

### Material

Define transparency, density, viscosity, turbulence, fracture, inclusions, surface roughness, emissive behavior, refraction, reflection, and scale. Distinguish matter from energy.

### Lighting interaction

Specify what the effect illuminates, shadows, reflects, refracts, occludes, or overexposes. Keep response local and consistent with ambient exposure.

### Physics reaction

Include secondary effects appropriate to scale: recoil, pressure, heat, condensation, frost, sparks, debris, droplets, steam, smoke, turbulence, sound delay, and force direction.

### Dissipation

Give the effect a lifecycle. It may fracture, melt, evaporate, cool, decay into sparks, lose ionization, disperse as smoke, or leave residue. Do not let it simply vanish.

## 3. Material systems

### Ice

Model ice as frozen water, not blue magic.

Precursor: local temperature falls; breath becomes faint mist; dew freezes; frost traces leaf veins; air condenses.

Formation: fine crystals gather like reverse snowfall; hexagonal structures establish a transparent nucleus; crystalline faces grow outward.

Material: mostly transparent; internal bubbles and freeze cracks; sharp facets; environmental blue-green reflection; white edge highlights; refraction and subtle spectral dispersion under direct sun. Do not make the body uniformly blue or self-luminous.

Aftermath: stress cracks follow force; fragments retain momentum; surfaces become wet; meltwater flashes into vapor when heated.

### Fire

Model fire as turbulent combustion, not an orange solid sphere.

Precursor: heat shimmer distorts the background; local air accelerates; hair, fabric, and leaves respond before the main release.

Formation: begin with a directed high-speed flame jet; expand through rolling, oxygen-fed turbulence. Use a white-yellow hot core, orange-red outer tongues, unstable edges, soot or smoke only when fuel and combustion conditions support it.

Lighting: produce local, dynamic warm reflections. In daylight, prioritize hot-core exposure, heat distortion, and nearby response over global illumination.

Aftermath: flame weakens into smaller tongues, sparks, hot gas, smoke, and residual heat.

### Electricity

Model electricity as branching discharge through ionized air or conductive material. Establish charge buildup, corona, path finding, sharp illumination, acoustic crack, and afterimage. Avoid smooth purple whips unless explicitly stylized.

### Spatial distortion

Model spatial effects through disrupted image propagation: lensing, parallax inconsistency, stretched reflections, delayed background motion, edge shear, and displaced matter. Avoid a flat black disk unless it is physically defined.

## 4. Collisions

Design collisions as two systems changing each other.

For ice entering fire:

1. flame near the contact point thins or is displaced;
2. the ice surface melts and liquid flashes into white steam;
3. thermal stress cracks propagate through the ice according to force;
4. steam pressure and impact redirect flame laterally;
5. fragments, droplets, sparks, and foliage follow the pressure wave;
6. sunlight catches translucent steam and ice fragments;
7. the cloud disperses and reveals wet, cooling residue.

Use a steam burst only at a scale supported by heat, water mass, confinement, and contact time. Do not default to a spherical fireball.

## 5. Daylight integration

Under hard sun:

- retain ambient exposure and shadow direction;
- show ice through reflection, refraction, condensation, and specular edges;
- show fire through its bright core, local reflections, turbulent silhouette, and heat shimmer;
- reduce the visibility of distant sparks;
- render backlit steam with translucent bright edges and changing density;
- let pressure move foliage and therefore animate dappled light.

## 6. Quality lock and negatives

Append when a model needs explicit enforcement:

> High-end cinematic practical VFX integrated with physically based CGI. Every supernatural effect has a fixed source, visible formation, material behavior, propagation path, contact response, environmental lighting, secondary physics, aftermath, and gradual dissipation. Preserve live-action exposure, scale, inertia, and compositing.

Target only observed failures with negatives. Useful candidates:

- no glowing magic object;
- no game-skill aura;
- no anime energy halo;
- no purposeless particle cloud;
- no instant finished manifestation;
- no excessive bloom;
- no plastic or uniformly colored material;
- no random spherical explosion;
- no beam without a stable source;
- no ignored environmental reaction;
- no effect that disappears without residue or dissipation.

## 7. Review checklist

- Is the source visible or clearly established?
- Does the environment react before, during, and after manifestation?
- Does the material respond to light as that material should?
- Does propagation follow a readable path and speed?
- Does contact change both interacting systems?
- Do fragments, pressure, sound, and performer reactions match force and timing?
- Does the effect leave an appropriate tail or residue?
- Could this plausibly be a photographed plate with integrated high-end CGI?
