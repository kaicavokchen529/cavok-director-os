## 1. Story and beat analysis

Reduce the scene to an operational sentence: **someone wants something, an obstacle changes the situation, and the scene exits on a new state**.

Extract:

- objective and obstacle;
- audience information versus character information;
- emotional state at entry and exit;
- reveal, reversal, attack, decision, or relationship change;
- continuity facts that cannot drift.

Divide the scene into beats only when intent, information, power, or physical action changes. Give each beat one visual verb: observe, search, notice, reveal, approach, threaten, evade, counter, recover, or resolve.

## 2. Geography and viewpoint

Create a mental overhead map before the shot list. Fix compass-like screen directions, elevations, distances, obstacles, entrances, and escape paths.

For every shot, answer:

- Who owns the viewpoint?
- What can that character know at this moment?
- What new fact does the cut reveal?
- Where are all subjects relative to the previous shot?

Treat a POV cut as an information contract. A shared subjective view may represent two characters observing from the same position; do not show their bodies unless a deliberate over-the-shoulder element is requested. When reversing viewpoint, first show the observer noticing, then cut to what they see.

Maintain the 180-degree line unless disorientation is intentional and motivated. Re-establish geography after large vertical moves, teleports, or rapid exchanges.

## 3. Character performance and blocking

Translate emotion into observable behavior: gaze order, breath, jaw tension, shoulder height, hand readiness, weight distribution, step length, and interpersonal distance.

Use realistic reaction order:

1. sensory cue;
2. eyes redirect;
3. breath or micro-freeze;
4. head and torso follow;
5. weight transfers;
6. action begins.

Preserve relationship blocking. If two partners begin leaning together, keep the contact until a defined trigger forces separation. Convert the release of contact into the first combat action so the transition feels practiced rather than staged.

Avoid symmetrical hero poses, simultaneous generic reactions, unsupported body angles, weightless landings, and hands that teleport between positions.

## 4. Camera language

Choose camera behavior only after diagnosing story function, audience information, viewpoint, relationship change, geography, and rhythm. Treat stillness as a deliberate option. Use signature movement only when removing it would weaken meaning, emotion, or spatial understanding.

Choose framing by information density:

- wide: geography, power relationship, path, or scale;
- medium-wide: full-body blocking and action readability;
- medium: relationship and tactical behavior;
- close-up: decision, recognition, or precise physical detail;
- insert: indispensable clue, contact point, or mechanism.

Choose movement by dramatic function:

- push: increasing attention or pressure;
- pull: isolation, consequence, or revealed context;
- track: follow spatial action;
- pan/tilt: discover information within a continuous space;
- arc: clarify relationship or change dominance;
- handheld: embodied instability, not generic excitement;
- locked frame: tension, observation, or performance authority.

Specify start, acceleration, deceleration, arrival, and hold. Let subjects move inside a stable composition when the scene is about observation. Do not use orbiting as decoration.

Use lens language cautiously. Prefer perceptual results—compressed distance, intimate proximity, controlled distortion, deep spatial reading—over arbitrary millimeter numbers unless production specifications are needed.

## 5. Environment and lighting

Define location as usable production space: floor conditions, structural supports, foreground occluders, vertical layers, weather, atmosphere, and surfaces that can react.

Motivate light from real sources. Track direction, hardness, color, exposure, shadow movement, reflections, and continuity across cuts. In bright daylight, fire creates local dynamic warmth and heat distortion; it should not turn the entire scene into orange night lighting. Transparent ice reflects sky and surroundings rather than glowing blue by default.

Let the environment participate: branches take weight, leaves move after pressure changes, dust rises after impacts, water condenses on cold surfaces, and moving foliage changes dappled light.

## 6. Action choreography

Design exchanges as intention and counter-intention, not random spectacle:

1. preparation;
2. readable attack line;
3. defense or evasion;
4. contact or near miss;
5. momentum transfer;
6. recovery and new tactical state.

Protect silhouettes and screen direction. Use wide or medium-wide coverage for spatially important moves; reserve close-ups for decisive contact, injury, or information. Show setup before payoff. Slow motion only when it reveals otherwise unreadable causality or emotion.

Integrate abilities with choreography. Define the caster's stance, source point, line of fire, partner clearance, recoil, environmental obstruction, and recovery.

## 7. Sound and dialogue

Build sound in layers:

- location bed and perspective;
- production Foley;
- character breath and cloth;
- ability source and material behavior;
- impact, debris, and tail;
- dialogue and intentional silence.

Use sound bridges to motivate cuts. Reduce ambience selectively before a reveal; do not mute the world without cause. Describe dialogue performance—distance, breath, restraint, overlap—rather than only the words.

## 8. Prompt construction

Order the prompt as the audience experiences the scene:

1. format and duration;
2. reality/style lock;
3. character and environment invariants;
4. timed chronological beats;
5. camera and focus behavior;
6. performance and blocking;
7. lighting, sound, and VFX interactions;
8. end frame and transition;
9. targeted negatives.

Use concrete verbs and visible states. Replace “dramatic confrontation” with the exact gaze, distance, movement, shadow, and sound that create it. Avoid contradictory camera instructions and overloading one time interval with too many actions.

## 9. Review and iteration

Audit the result in this order:

1. story and viewpoint clarity;
2. geography and screen direction;
3. identity and wardrobe continuity;
4. blocking and body mechanics;
5. camera motivation and timing;
6. lighting continuity;
7. VFX source, material, interaction, and lifecycle;
8. sound causality;
9. unwanted model artifacts.

Preserve successful composition and performance when the failure is isolated to VFX. Revise the minimum dependent layers necessary. Convert recurring failures into concise negative constraints or reusable rules.
