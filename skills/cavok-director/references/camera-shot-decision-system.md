## 1. Decision order

Do not begin with a favorite movement or famous shot. Decide in this order:

```text
dramatic purpose
→ required audience information
→ viewpoint ownership
→ relationship or power change
→ geography and action direction
→ emotional distance and rhythm
→ shot size, height, angle, lens, focus, movement, duration, and cut
```

Ask: if this technique is removed, does story clarity, emotion, relationship, or spatial meaning weaken? If not, it is probably decoration.

## 2. Content-to-camera strategy

| Content | Camera priority | Useful starting choices |
|---|---|---|
| Natural conversation | preserve relationship and performance | eye-level two-shot, medium coverage, restrained reframing |
| Intimacy | proximity, permission, shared space | close two-shot, shallow depth only when isolation helps, gentle approach |
| Power negotiation | dominance and access | foreground hierarchy, height, frame share, controlled push or lateral reveal |
| Investigation | discovery order | POV, inserts, rack focus, foreground reveal, observational movement |
| Suspense | known versus unknown | locked frames, negative space, occlusion, delayed reveal, slow controlled movement |
| Fear or realization | altered perception | reaction-first push, selective dolly zoom, narrowed sound perspective |
| Grief or farewell | duration and absence | stable long take, distant framing, negative space, delayed cut or withdrawal |
| Comedy | setup, timing, reaction | stable wide, clean entrances, preserved pause, reaction coverage |
| Awe | scale and discovery | reveal, vertical tilt, wide spatial contrast, reaction before spectacle when appropriate |
| Travel or transition | direction and scale | moving wide, aerial geography, route continuity |
| Chase | route, speed, obstacle | front/side follow, long-lens compression, aerial reset, clear screen direction |
| Action | causality, force, consequence | spatial anchor, readable master, selective fast cuts, contact and result shots |
| Aftermath | cost and changed state | stable wide/medium, slow withdrawal, environmental residue |

## 3. Shot size and height

Use wide shots for geography, scale, isolation, paths, and power relationships. Use full and medium-wide shots for body mechanics and blocking. Use medium shots for relationships and tactical decisions. Use close-ups for irreversible decisions, recognition, or necessary detail. Use extreme close-ups sparingly for sensory or mechanical emphasis.

Do not cut to close-up merely because dialogue is important. Close framing removes environmental evidence and magnifies performance.

Use camera height deliberately:

- eye level: neutral access, relationship, readable exchange;
- low angle: dominance, vertical force, elevation, threat, monumentality;
- high angle: vulnerability, pattern, surveillance, confinement;
- ground level: feet, sliding, falling objects, impacts, near-lens passes;
- overhead: routes, formations, geometry, bodies, state change.

Avoid equating low angle with heroism or high angle with weakness automatically. Blocking, lens, distance, light, and context may reverse the effect.

## 4. Action direction and camera position

| Action | Primary readable position | Optional emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal sweep | lateral medium-wide | low-angle near pass for pressure and weapon silhouette |
| Downward strike | three-quarter or lateral full body | defender POV/low angle for vertical threat |
| Upward cut | low three-quarter side | contact insert if decisive |
| Thrust | slight offset along attack axis | target-side compression for approach |
| Spinning kick | medium-wide preserving rotation axis | low angle only if feet and target remain separated |
| Leg sweep | ground-level lateral | wider result for fall and new positions |
| Leap or drop attack | wide low angle showing height and landing | impact detail and aftermath |
| Throw | three-quarter full body | lateral flight path and landing |
| Knockback | lateral wide | foreground debris or compressed long lens for force |
| Charge | frontal retreat or lateral track | long lens for collision pressure |

For a high horizontal sweep, use a low angle when the dramatic goal is dominance, weapon scale, or overhead danger and the target remains readable. Prefer the lateral master when technique, clearance, and evade path matter. Combine them rather than forcing one shot to do both jobs.

## 5. Movement grammar

- locked frame: observation, inevitability, performance authority, comedy timing, or waiting;
- push: increasing attention, pressure, recognition, or commitment;
- pull: isolation, consequence, release, revealed context, or ending relationship;
- lateral track: relationship, route, body silhouette, or sequential reveal;
- pan/tilt: discover connected information in continuous space;
- arc: reveal relationship or dominance changing, not generic energy;
- handheld: embodied instability or urgency with controlled recovery;
- whip pan: sudden attention transfer or attack redirection with a precise landing;
- roll or body-mounted view: exceptional subjective disorientation;
- zoom: optical emphasis or perceptual change, distinct from physical camera travel.

Specify motivation, start cue, acceleration, travel, deceleration, landing, and hold. Stillness is a designed camera behavior.

## 6. Lens and focus

Use perceptual goals:

- wide and close: embodied proximity, exaggerated depth, environmental speed, controlled distortion;
- normal perspective: balanced relationship and readable blocking;
- long lens: surveillance, isolation, compressed collision, layered crowds, or distant threat;
- shallow focus: restricted attention or emotional isolation;
- deeper focus: geography, ensemble relationships, suspense within the frame;
- rack focus: transfer information or power without cutting;
- split focus/deep staging: simultaneous foreground and background conflict.

Use slow zoom for accumulating attention or unease. Use crash zoom for abrupt type-driven emphasis. Use dolly zoom only when perception, threat, or psychological space changes; do not use it as a generic surprise effect.

## 7. Aerial and drone language

Use aerial movement to solve macro information:

- establish geography, scale, elevation, route, formation, or isolation;
- follow sustained high-speed travel;
- reveal encirclement or battlefield transformation;
- bridge from landscape to character when the movement has a destination and settles.

Do not default every scene to an aerial opener. Avoid drones for nuanced facial performance, close combat mechanics, confined occluded interiors, or sequences already overloaded with moving cameras.

An aerial shot must answer: what spatial fact becomes clearer from above? Define subject, route, altitude, direction, parallax, arrival, and exit. After descending toward characters, stabilize or hand off to a grounded camera language.

## 8. Classical shot tools

Treat established techniques as functions, not imitation:

| Tool | Function | Misuse warning |
|---|---|---|
| Blocking-driven oner | evolve space and relationships without cutting | empty continuous movement |
| Reaction push | make discovery or realization the event | push with no internal change |
| Foreground reveal | uncover hidden information through parallax | decorative object wipe |
| Dolly zoom | rupture perceived space or threat | routine surprise punctuation |
| Crash zoom | abrupt type-film emphasis | tonal mismatch or repeated gimmick |
| Trunk/container low POV | assign control to figures above a motivated viewpoint | homage without story logic |
| Standoff inserts | distribute threat through eyes, hands, weapons, and targets | details without spatial master |
| One-point perspective | order, ritual, entrapment, irreversible approach | symmetry without thematic use |
| Symmetry break | make disruption visibly violate order | random off-centering |
| Weather-driven motion | show force, depth, and atmosphere | unrelated particle decoration |
| Long-lens compression | make converging movement feel dangerous | hiding true distance when it matters |
| Stable action master | prove technique, timing, contact, and reaction | flat coverage with no emphasis |
| Low hero arc | mark genuine status transition or reveal | granting every entrance equal importance |
| Match cut or sound bridge | connect action, idea, time, or place | transition that confuses continuity |

Do not describe output as a direct copy of a living filmmaker. Name the underlying camera function and adapt it to the scene's own tone.

## 9. Coverage and audit

Build a shot group from complementary duties:

1. establish or anchor geography;
2. direct attention or viewpoint;
3. show performance or action clearly;
4. emphasize decisive detail or force;
5. confirm reaction, consequence, and new state.

Not every scene needs all five as separate shots. A blocking-driven oner may perform several duties if each transition remains readable.

```text
[ ] Camera choice follows content diagnosis.
[ ] Viewpoint and audience knowledge are explicit.
[ ] Shot size preserves required information.
[ ] Height and angle express the intended relationship without hiding action.
[ ] Movement begins for a reason and settles.
[ ] Lens and focus support spatial and emotional distance.
[ ] Signature techniques are earned, not decorative.
[ ] Fast cutting activates only when the beat benefits.
[ ] Coverage includes consequence and an edit-ready exit.
```
