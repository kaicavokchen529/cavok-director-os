# CAVOK 2D Anime Combat Grammar

## Purpose

Use this module when the target is hand-drawn 2D anime combat, 2D-styled action, sakuga-like key animation, or a hybrid pipeline whose action language is fundamentally graphic rather than physically photographed.

Do not treat this mode as live-action choreography with a cel-shading filter. The directing logic changes from continuous physical recording to **designed key poses, variable timing, selective deformation, graphic impact, hand-drawn FX, and controlled background abstraction**.

Core principle:

> Action must read before it dazzles. Pose establishes intent, timing creates speed, contact changes bodies, deformation proves force, and FX only reveal force that already exists.

This module is generic. Do not copy a named anime, studio, director, or episode shot-for-shot. Extract observable principles—pose design, spacing, timing, line hierarchy, impact treatment, background simplification, and camera function—and adapt them to the current characters and story.

---

## 1. Mode switch

When 2D anime combat is active, change the priority stack.

Live-action priority tends toward:

```text
blocking → physical causality → camera → optics → VFX integration
```

2D anime combat priority becomes:

```text
tactical intent
→ attack vector
→ key pose
→ silhouette
→ timing / spacing
→ contact
→ deformation
→ consequence pose
→ FX drawing
→ camera / edit
```

Physics still matters as causal logic, but exact real-world interpolation may be sacrificed for readability, rhythm, graphic force, or expressive distortion.

Do not sacrifice:

- who attacks and why;
- screen direction and attack axis;
- readable contact;
- tactical state change;
- character identity;
- consequence and recovery.

---

## 2. Core action lifecycle

Use this as the default beat structure:

```text
Read Pose
→ Anticipation
→ Launch
→ Burst / Smear
→ Contact
→ Impact Frame / Hit Hold when earned
→ Consequence Pose
→ Recovery
→ Re-engage or Exit
```

The audience does not need every centimeter of motion. Preserve the states that prove intention, release, contact, result, and changed advantage.

### Read Pose

Establish current stance, facing, weapon line, distance, and tactical relationship.

### Anticipation

Compress the body before release. Examples:

- center of gravity drops;
- shoulder or pelvis loads in the opposite direction;
- blade retracts;
- fist pulls back;
- spine twists;
- support foot turns or grips.

Anticipation tells the audience where force comes from.

### Launch

Show the first unmistakable release frame. Direction must be clear in silhouette.

### Burst / Smear

Use one or several extreme transition drawings rather than smooth uniform interpolation when the action peaks.

### Contact

Keep attacker, target, contact point, and force direction legible. FX must not erase the collision.

### Consequence Pose

Show the new physical state: knockback, collapse, slide, bend, recoil, regained footing, broken guard, changed distance, or altered advantage.

### Recovery

Hair, cloth, limbs, weapons, breath, and balance continue after the main action. Avoid instant neutral-pose reset.

---

## 3. Key-pose system

Protect six pose classes.

### Neutral / Read Pose

Readable staging and current tactical state. Not every read pose must be a hero pose.

### Anticipation Pose

Maximum compression before release. Strong counter-direction is useful when it clarifies stored force.

### Launch Pose

The spring releases. Use a clear diagonal, weight shift, or line of action.

### Extreme / Smear Pose

Temporary graphic deformation at speed peak. Character identity may simplify, but motion direction must become clearer, not noisier.

### Contact Pose

The collision frame. Preserve contact geometry and force hierarchy.

### Consequence Pose

The state after the hit. This is mandatory for important exchanges; without it, attacks feel consequence-free.

### Silhouette test

Temporarily imagine each key pose as a solid black shape. If the viewer cannot read attacker, defender, attack limb/weapon, force direction, and result, repair the pose before adding FX.

---

## 4. Timing and spacing

2D action power comes from unequal timing, not constant speed.

Default rhythm:

```text
Hold
→ Anticipation
→ Burst
→ Contact
→ very short Hit Hold if earned
→ Release
→ Recovery
```

Use stillness as stored energy. Before a fast action, permit only minor movement such as eyes, breath, hair tips, cloth edges, smoke, or environmental motion.

### Variable exposure feeling

When the pipeline supports it, think in terms of variable frame density:

- **On Twos feeling**: calmer motion, readable staging, ordinary movement, dialogue, setup;
- **On Ones feeling**: acceleration peak, smear, weapon travel, impact, high-speed exchange;
- **Held drawing**: anticipation or impact emphasis.

Do not mechanically animate the whole sequence on the same temporal density.

### Hit hold

A major contact may hold visually for roughly 1–3 frame equivalents, but ordinary attacks should not all receive hit stop. The hold exists to let the collision register, not to imitate game feedback.

---

## 5. Speed hierarchy

Treat speed as a ladder. Use only the level needed by the dramatic beat.

### Level 1 — Body Speed

Pose, spacing, and ordinary motion blur carry the movement.

### Level 2 — Smear

Directional deformation appears at the motion peak.

### Level 3 — Follow Lines

Short lines follow limbs, weapons, cloth, or the attack arc.

### Level 4 — Afterimage

Low-detail temporal echoes mark previous positions. They are not clones.

### Level 5 — Background Speed

The environment reduces to directional streaks, compressed perspective, or large graphic planes.

### Level 6 — Temporal Omission

Do not show the whole travel path. Show:

```text
launch
→ missing / unreadable travel
→ delayed environmental response
→ clear new position
→ readable contact or result
```

Use Level 6 for genuinely exceptional speed, not normal combat.

---

## 6. Smear and deformation grammar

Smear is a controlled drawing tool, not anatomy failure.

### Limb Smear

Stretch or simplify a fist, forearm, foot, or leg along the true movement vector.

### Weapon Smear

Allow a blade, staff, chain, or weapon path to become a short fan, wedge, arc, or stretched silhouette while preserving the underlying weapon identity before and after the peak.

### Body Smear

Compress, widen, stretch, or bend the whole figure for a very short transition when the motion demands it.

### Face Smear

Use only for violent head motion, sudden turn, or contact deformation. Return immediately to the approved face model.

### Shape Smear

At extreme speed, a body may briefly collapse into an abstract wedge, arc, streak, or silhouette if that abstraction makes direction clearer.

### Contact deformation

Force should travel through material and anatomy. Examples:

- face compresses before head turns;
- torso bends before whole-body displacement;
- sleeve or coat stretches after the skeleton changes direction;
- weapon vibrates after the primary block;
- debris moves after contact, not before.

Do not leave stretched anatomy permanently altered after the speed peak.

---

## 7. Speed-line library

Do not use a single generic speed-line treatment.

### Directional Speed Lines

Parallel or convergent lines that communicate translation. Align them with the dominant vector.

### Radial Impact Lines

Burst outward from a contact or shock center. Use for impact, not ordinary travel.

### Convergence / Pressure Lines

Lines pull inward toward a subject or strike point. Use for threat, charging pressure, or pre-impact compression.

### Arc Lines

Follow the true curve of punches, kicks, blades, staffs, tails, or rotating bodies.

### Rough Brush Streaks

Dry-brush, broken ink, flying-white, rough painted streaks, or irregular slabs that increase violence and hand-drawn texture.

### Line hierarchy

Use three levels:

- **Primary force line**: thickest, fewest, defines the main vector;
- **Secondary motion lines**: support local movement and arcs;
- **Texture lines**: fine irregular breakup, lowest visual priority.

More lines do not automatically create more speed. Direction and hierarchy matter more than density.

---

## 8. Afterimage rules

Afterimages are visual memory, not duplicated bodies.

Use:

- fewer than needed rather than too many;
- lower detail than the current figure;
- progressively reduced opacity or broken contour;
- poses that correspond to meaningful previous action states;
- spacing that reflects acceleration.

Avoid equally detailed copies, identical spacing, duplicated weapons, and persistent ghost trails after the subject stops.

---

## 9. Impact-frame hierarchy

Impact frames emphasize collision by temporarily changing graphic treatment. They do not replace contact.

### Tier 1 — Local Impact

Contact deformation, a few burst lines, material sparks, dust, or hand-drawn accent shapes.

### Tier 2 — Strong Impact

High-contrast linework, partial desaturation or inversion, rough stroke burst, stronger local deformation.

### Tier 3 — Graphic Impact

Brief abstraction such as:

- black/white inversion;
- rough pencil or charcoal;
- ink splash;
- red-black silhouette;
- isolated flat-color field;
- extreme contour drawing.

### Tier 4 — Signature Impact

A climactic attack may briefly shift visual medium or rendering logic. Exact contact registration, pose, and force direction must remain stable across the transformation.

Do not give every hit Tier 3–4 treatment. Preserve escalation space.

---

## 10. Hand-drawn FX system

Treat stylized effects as **FX drawings**, not generic particle decoration.

### Energy FX

Graphic arcs, bands, cores, blades, pressure forms, or constrained energy shapes tied to a source.

### Impact FX

Starbursts, jagged spikes, brush explosions, contact wedges, crack lines.

### Debris FX

Rocks, splinters, dust chunks, dirt, leaves, droplets. Large readable pieces move first; fine material follows.

### Smoke / Pressure FX

Designed smoke shapes, pressure clouds, impact rings, suction lines, wake forms.

### Fire FX

Hand-drawn flame shapes with changing silhouettes and a readable hot core; avoid uniform orange blobs.

### Electricity FX

Irregular branching paths, broken timing, asymmetric forks. Avoid smooth decorative neon whips unless character design requires it.

### Aura FX

Reserve for states that matter. Do not keep every fighter inside a permanent aura.

### FX lifecycle

Every important FX event should answer:

```text
Source → Direction → Shape → Peak → Breakup → Dissipation / Residual
```

FX must support the action vector and character identity.

---

## 11. Background-state system

Background detail is allowed to change with action intensity.

### BG-0 — Full Environment

Complete location and geography. Use for establishing space, confrontation, tactics, consequence, and recovery.

### BG-1 — Reduced Detail

Keep large spatial forms and landmarks while reducing texture. Use during fast exchanges when geography still matters.

### BG-2 — Motion Background

Replace much of the environment with directional lines, elongated color fields, warped perspective, or simplified planes. Use for major translation.

### BG-3 — Impact Void

The normal background temporarily disappears. Keep only subjects, contact, graphic field, and impact drawing. Use only for major beats.

### Return rule

After BG-2 or BG-3, return to a readable spatial anchor so the audience can confirm distance, direction, damage, and advantage.

Do not let abstract backgrounds erase battlefield geography for long stretches.

---

## 12. Perspective exaggeration

2D combat may temporarily exaggerate perspective for force:

- enlarged fist, foot, weapon, or foreground limb near camera;
- stretched depth between attacker and target;
- sharp foreshortening;
- extreme near/far size difference;
- graphic lens-like distortion not possible in strict live action.

The exaggeration must strengthen the attack vector and pose. Restore stable proportions after the beat. Do not confuse expressive perspective with uncontrolled anatomy drift.

---

## 13. Camera language

Use two camera categories.

### Physical Camera

Relatively grounded spatial camera. Use for geography, tactics, pre-action tension, readable technique, and consequence.

### Graphic Camera

Stylized 2D viewpoint changes such as extreme foreshortening, rapid reorientation, aggressive low/high angle, snap reframing, impossible-but-readable perspective travel, or compositional whip.

Default rhythm:

```text
Stable spatial camera
→ action burst
→ graphic emphasis when earned
→ contact
→ stable result anchor
```

Do not combine maximum character speed, maximum camera speed, maximum rotation, and maximum FX in the same beat unless deliberate perceptual overload is the story point.

---

## 14. Editing grammar

Cut on information and force changes, not on arbitrary duration.

Useful cut points:

1. decision / gaze lock;
2. support foot or grip load;
3. launch;
4. vector change;
5. defense reveal;
6. contact;
7. momentum transfer;
8. consequence / advantage shift.

Fast inserts may show eyes, foot plant, hand grip, weapon edge, wound, or mechanism, but each insert must add indispensable information.

Avoid replaying the same attack from several decorative angles. Protect setup, contact, and result.

---

## 15. Spatial continuity

Stylization does not excuse spatial collapse.

Before a high-speed passage, establish:

- left/right positions;
- facing;
- relative distance;
- elevation;
- attack axis;
- obstacles and exits.

During abstraction, preserve screen direction or provide a motivated axis reset. After major teleport-like speed, vertical displacement, knockback, or BG-3 impact, return to a spatial anchor.

---

## 16. Character performance

2D combat still requires acting. Do not reduce all fighters to shouting, clenched teeth, and angry brows.

Direct:

- gaze target and gaze change;
- breath;
- jaw and mouth tension;
- brow and eyelid behavior;
- shoulder height;
- hand readiness;
- spine and pelvis;
- support foot;
- confidence, hesitation, calculation, fear, delight, restraint, or exhaustion as visible behavior.

Strong characters often become more compelling when the face remains controlled while the body action becomes extreme.

---

## 17. Character Combat Signature

Each major fighter should have a distinct animation/action language.

Record:

```text
Primary line of action: straight / curved / broken / heavy / flowing
Preferred range:
Rhythm: patient / burst / continuous / feint-heavy / counter-based
Pose character:
Smear character:
Speed-line character:
Impact-frame intensity:
FX material / brush language:
Background-abstraction tendency:
Recovery style:
Emotional combat mask:
```

Examples of contrasting signatures:

- **Heavy linear fighter**: few moves, strong holds, broad primary lines, thick impact strokes, large consequence poses;
- **Agile curved fighter**: frequent vector changes, S-curves, lighter linework, selective afterimages, fluid recoveries;
- **Violent explosive fighter**: large pose changes, aggressive smear, frequent BG reduction, high deformation, but signature impact reserved for key hits.

Do not give every character the same crescent trail, smear shape, and impact treatment.

---

## 18. Power / emphasis tiers

Protect escalation with four visual levels.

### Tier 1 — Normal

Pose + readable motion + contact + small local FX.

### Tier 2 — Strong

Smear + impact lines + clear deformation + stronger consequence.

### Tier 3 — Hero

Background reduction + variable timing + stronger smear + impact frame + major deformation.

### Tier 4 — Signature / Finisher

Temporal omission or extreme spacing + graphic camera + high-level impact frame + strong environmental consequence + longer recovery/residual.

Do not use Tier 4 language for ordinary exchanges.

---

## 19. Sound for 2D combat

Stylized image does not require generic exaggerated audio. Preserve causal layering:

```text
preparation / cloth / breath
→ attack whoosh
→ optional ambience reduction
→ contact transient
→ body / weapon / material hit
→ debris / environment reaction
→ resonance / reverb / tail
```

A brief reduction of sound before a decisive impact can increase perceived force. Avoid one continuous loud wall of sound.

---

## 20. Color and graphic hierarchy

High-energy 2D action benefits from disciplined color.

For emissive or supernatural FX use:

```text
small bright core
→ main shape color
→ edge / brush breakup
→ lower-energy residual
```

Keep character identity colors readable. Do not permanently wash the whole frame in one energy color.

Black, white, and one restrained accent color can often create a stronger impact frame than many equally saturated colors.

---

## 21. AI execution rules

When generating 2D anime combat with AI, do not lead with vague phrases such as `insane sakuga`, `crazy fight`, or `speed lines everywhere`.

Prioritize:

```text
Character identity
→ Start pose / incoming state
→ Attack vector
→ Key pose sequence
→ Timing change
→ Contact point
→ Consequence pose
→ FX drawing
→ Background state
→ End state
```

### One major graphic escalation per beat

Do not simultaneously maximize:

- pose deformation;
- camera rotation;
- speed-line density;
- afterimage count;
- impact-frame abstraction;
- background destruction.

Choose a dominant device for each beat.

### Prompt separation

Separate:

- subject motion;
- camera motion;
- background-state change;
- FX behavior.

### Identity protection

Smear and abstraction may simplify the character temporarily, but approved face, costume, weapon, proportions, handedness, and distinguishing features must restore immediately after the extreme frame.

### End-state protection

End on a stable readable pose/state whenever the clip must seed the next generation.

If the target model is Seedance 2.5, design the action with this module first, then translate the approved beats through `seedance-2.5.md`.

## 22. Beat template

Use this compact format for 2D action design:

```text
[Time / Beat]
Dramatic purpose:
Framing / composition:
Camera:
Incoming state:
Key pose:
Timing: Hold / Anticipation / Burst / Contact / Recovery
Attack / defense vector:
Smear / afterimage:
FX lines:
Impact tier:
Background state: BG-0 / BG-1 / BG-2 / BG-3
Deformation / consequence:
Sound:
Outgoing state:
```

Not every beat needs every field. Use only what affects the result.

---

## 23. Failure modes and negatives

Avoid:

- uniformly smooth interpolation across every action;
- equal-speed motion with no hold/burst contrast;
- speed lines with the wrong direction;
- afterimages that become full-detail clones;
- permanent anatomy deformation after a smear;
- FX covering the actual contact point;
- every attack receiving a flash, hit stop, or signature frame;
- every weapon creating the same crescent trail;
- background remaining abstract so long that geography disappears;
- random camera rotation combined with unreadable body rotation;
- no consequence pose after contact;
- characters instantly returning to neutral pose;
- explosion before contact;
- visual noise used to hide poor pose design;
- every fighter sharing the same animation signature;
- generic permanent aura;
- named-style imitation replacing actual directing decisions.

---

## 24. QC checklist

Before approving a 2D combat beat, ask:

```text
[ ] If all FX are removed, is the action readable from silhouette and pose alone?
[ ] Are attacker, defender, vector, contact, and consequence clear?
[ ] Does speed come from timing/spacing rather than blur alone?
[ ] Is anticipation strong enough to justify the release?
[ ] Does the smear exist only where speed peaks?
[ ] Does impact treatment correspond to real contact?
[ ] Do FX lines follow actual force direction?
[ ] Does deformation propagate through the body/material in order?
[ ] Does the background return to readable geography after abstraction?
[ ] Is the result pose held long enough to register changed advantage?
[ ] Does each major fighter have a distinct combat signature?
[ ] Is the strongest visual treatment reserved for the strongest dramatic beat?
[ ] Are identity and costume restored cleanly after extreme drawings?
[ ] Can the outgoing state seed the next beat or clip without ambiguity?
```

---

## Final principle

> **先立 Pose，再定方向；先压时间，再放速度；接触必须改变身体；FX 只描绘力量；背景可以为动作让路，但空间不能丢。**

The goal is not maximum visual noise. The goal is designed force: the viewer should understand the action first, feel the impact second, and remember the pose and consequence afterward.
